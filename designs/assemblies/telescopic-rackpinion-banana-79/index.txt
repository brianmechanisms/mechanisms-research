3:T8d4,Current build: six concentric curved ring sectors (rings 0..5) arranged in a planetary cascade. Even rings (0, 2, 4) sit in one Z-slab; odd rings (1, 3, 5) sit in the offset slab. Intermediate rings (1..4) carry teeth on both arcs and two pinions each, mounted on shafts on the exposed end face that faces toward the next ring out. Ring 0 is fixed; driving ring 1 cascades through ring 5 with each successive ring sweeping faster than the last.

Possible applications:
1. Deployable tent — not sure it strictly fits the "deployable mechanism" definition, but the fan-out of nested arcs could form a self-spreading curved canopy.
2. Fancy gate — the cascading sweep gives a dramatic opening motion across a curved opening.
3. Fancy door — same idea, vertical orientation, panels nest and fan out as the door opens.
4. Door opening/closing mechanism — a compact actuator that multiplies a small input sweep into a large output sweep via the planetary ratio at each stage.

To investigate — force / actuator-sizing advantage over linear actuators:
A conventional linear actuator on a door or gate pushes along a fixed line, but the door swings on an arc. The actuator force has to be decomposed into a tangential component (which does useful work moving the door) and a radial component (absorbed by the hinge as wasted capacity). The decomposition is geometry-dependent and worst near the fully open / fully closed positions, so the actuator must be sized for the WORST tangential-fraction in its travel — i.e. you end up specifying a more powerful actuator than the average required torque would suggest.

This banana cascade in principle applies its output torque directly to the swinging member through the gear mesh, with no force decomposed away into the hinge. Combined with the planetary speed multiplication (input small sweep → output large sweep), the input torque required should likewise be reduced by the overall ratio, so a smaller / less powerful actuator could in principle achieve the same door motion.
