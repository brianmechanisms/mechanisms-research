Long-Range Linear Motion Mechanism Using a Pair of Out-of-Phase Compound Epicyclic Gear Trains with Linear-Transition Racetrack Paths

Introduction

This paper presents a mechanism for producing long-range linear motion using compound epicyclic gear trains that travel around racetrack-shaped closed tracks. Unlike conventional epicyclic systems which produce purely rotary output, the racetrack track geometry introduces straight sections where the gear train translates linearly, enabling direct linear actuation. Two such gear trains, operating 180° out of phase on the same track, provide continuous gripping — at any instant at least one gear train is in its working stroke.

The Compound Epicyclic Gear Train

The gear train consists of four gears arranged in two meshing pairs. A compound gear with two coaxial faces of radii r1 (outer, larger) and r2 (inner, smaller), rigidly connected on a common shaft, forms the central element. The gear ratio k = r1/r2 > 1 determines the quick-return speed ratio. Two driven gears complete the train: r3 meshes with the compound’s outer face r1 and rolls on the outer track, while r4 meshes with the inner face r2 and rolls on the inner track.

The track is a racetrack shape: two parallel straight racks of length L connected at each end by semicircular arcs. The outer semicircular radius is R1 = r1 + 2r3 and the inner semicircular radius is R2 = r2 + 2r4. The two tracks are concentric at the semicircular ends and parallel on the straight sections.

Only one driven gear engages its rack during each straight section, while both driven gears engage their respective semicircular rings simultaneously at the turns. The mechanism completes a four-phase cycle per revolution of the track:

Phase Path Active gear
StanceBottom rack →r4 on inner rack
LiftRight semicircler3 and r4 both
SwingTop rack ←r3 on outer rack
DropLeft semicircler3 and r4 both

The compound gear rotates at angular velocity ωc. From the gear ratio at each mesh, the driven gear angular velocities are ω3 = ωc·r1/r3 and ω4 = ωc·r2/r4.

During stance, driven gear r4 rolls without slip on the inner rack. Since r4 rotates at ω4 = ωc·r2/r4, the driven gear centre translates at v = ω4·r4 = ωc·r2. The compound centre, at fixed spacing from the driven gear, translates at the same velocity. By identical reasoning, during swing the compound centre translates at v = ωc·r1. The stance and swing durations for a rack of length L are therefore:

tstance = Lωc·r2 , tswing = Lωc·r1 (1)

Since r1 > r2, the swing is faster than the stance — producing the quick-return effect.

At the semicircular turns, the driven gears orbit around fixed semicircular rings, forming a planetary gear train. The compound gear centre acts as the carrier (arm). The Willis equation, the fundamental equation of planetary gear trains, relates sun, ring, and arm angular velocities for any simple epicyclic stage:

ωsun−ωarm ωring−ωarm = − rring rsun (2)

For the outer gear pair (sun = r1, planet = r3, ring = R1 fixed), setting ωring = 0 and solving for the arm angular velocity gives ωa = ωc·r1/(r1 + R1). Since R1 = r1 + 2r3, it follows that:

ωa,outer = ωc·r1 2(r1+r3) (3)

By identical reasoning for the inner gear pair (sun = r2, planet = r4, ring = R2 fixed):

ωa,inner = ωc·r2 2(r2+r4) (4)

Since both driven gears share the same arm (the compound gear centre), the arm angular velocity must be identical for both gear–ring pairs at the turns. Setting ωa,outer = ωa,inner and cross-multiplying yields the simultaneity constraint:

r1 · r4 = r2 · r3 (5)

The two semicircular turns are geometrically identical — both have the same ring radii R1 and R2 with the same gear pairs engaged. The lift and drop durations are therefore equal. Each turn sweeps π radians at angular velocity ωa, giving:

tlift = tdrop = π ωa = 2π(r1+r3) ωc·r1 (6)

The total cycle time ttotal = tstance + tlift + tswing + tdrop, and ωc cancels throughout. The stance fraction is:

f = tstance ttotal = Lr2 Lr2 + 4π(r1+r3) r1 + Lr1 (7)

The stance fraction depends only on the gear radii and rack length. Solving (7) for L in terms of a desired stance fraction f yields the minimum rack length:

Lmin = 4πf(r1+r3) k(1−f)−f (8)

where k = r1/r2. The denominator vanishes when f = k/(k+1), which represents the maximum achievable stance fraction — the limit as L → ∞, where the turns become negligible relative to the straights. For any finite mechanism, f must be strictly less than k/(k+1).

Kinematic Inversions

The gear train and racetrack track form a single kinematic chain with one degree of freedom. Two useful mechanisms arise by choosing which body to fix (ground):

Configuration Grounded body Moving body Output
Racetrack Fixed Racetrack frame Compound gear translates around track Point on compound gear (Q)
Compound Fixed Compound gear shaft Racetrack translates past compound Racetrack linear displacement

Both configurations produce the same relative motion between compound gear and track — they are kinematic inversions of each other. The velocities, phase durations, and gear-tooth forces are identical; only the observer’s frame of reference changes. The choice between them depends on the application: racetrack-fixed suits cases where the output must be taken from a moving point (e.g. a pick-and-place arm), while compound-fixed suits cases where the output is the linear displacement of the track itself (e.g. a bed or carriage).

The Bell Crank Configuration

In the racetrack-fixed inversion, a point Q on the compound gear’s radial arm serves as the output. A rigid bell-crank arm connects Q through a slider on a horizontal rail to a gripper. The slider’s horizontal position tracks Q’s x-coordinate.

For continuous motion, two gear trains operate 180° out of phase on the same track, so that at least one is always in its stance phase.

The Slotted Slider Configuration

In this configuration the slider is the body (ground) and the gripper is mounted on the racetrack rack section, which now moves relative to the slider body. The output point Q drives the slider through a vertical slot, translating Q’s horizontal motion directly to the body.

Since the body (slider) is common to both gear trains, each compound gear sees the same relative motion with respect to the body regardless of phase. The two compound gears can therefore be connected by rigid links and driven by a single actuator — they maintain fixed relative positions because their motion relative to the moving body is identical. This makes the slotted slider the preferred configuration for continuous-motion applications.

The Compound-Fixed Configuration

In the compound-fixed inversion, the compound gear shaft is grounded — it rotates in place but does not translate. The racetrack track slides past the compound gear, producing direct linear output from the track’s translation. During stance the track translates at v = ωc·r2; during swing it returns at v = ωc·r1.

This configuration is mechanically simpler: the output is the track’s own displacement, requiring no intermediate linkage. The compound gear sits on a fixed bearing, eliminating the need for a carrier or slider mechanism.

Velocity-Continuous Output

Point P sits on the compound gear’s radial arm at a variable radius dP. During stance, dP = R2; during swing, dP = R1. During each turn, P’s radius transitions via sinusoidal interpolation where θ sweeps from π/2 to −π/2:

dP(θ) = R1+R22 − R1−R22 ·sin(θ) (9)

Point Q extends the constant-velocity region into the turns by an angle φ. Within this hold zone, Q maintains vx = ωc·r2 while the compound gear rotates around the semicircular arc. With the radial arm at angle θ, the horizontal velocity of a point at varying radius d is vx = ωc[d·sin(θ) − d′·cos(θ)]. Setting vx = ωc·R2 and integrating with the boundary condition d(π/2) = R2 gives:

d(θ)= R2·(π2−θ)cos(θ) (10)

At θ = π/2 this evaluates via L’Hôpital’s rule to d = R2, confirming continuity. At the boundary between the hold zone (angle π/2 − φ) and the transition zone, both the radius and its derivative are continuous. A cubic Hermite polynomial interpolates from the hold zone endpoint to (R1, 0) over the remaining angular span π − φ, ensuring C1 continuity of the velocity profile (design aim 4).
