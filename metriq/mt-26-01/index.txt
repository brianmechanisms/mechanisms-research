3:T13d1,## Intent

Two vertical tubes side by side, joined into one body by a middle wall that
stops short of the bottom. A single slanting plunger reciprocates below that
wall. Turn the drive and solids move from tube **B** into tube **A**.

No valves, no timing, no separate sequencing element. The direction comes from
one inclined face and one screen.

## The parts

| # | Part | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Left tube — the **receiving** side | Fixed. Open at the bottom. |
| **B** | Right tube — the **supply** side | Fixed. Open at the bottom. |
| **middle wall** | Divides A from B, **stops short of the bottom** | Fixed. Its bottom edge is the plunger's top stop. |
| **screen** | Spans A, level with the middle wall's bottom edge | Fixed. The one-way element. |
| **plunger** | One slanting member spanning **both** bores | One DOF: vertical translation. |

There is **no floor**. The tubes are open at the bottom and the plunger *is*
the floor — which is why its lowest position is a hard limit rather than a
preference.

## The slant

One continuous inclined face, **highest in B and falling the whole way into
A**. No level section, no ridge. Everything resting on it slides down and to
the left — out of B, across the crossover, and into A. That single inclination
is the entire direction-giving mechanism.

The face must be steeper than the material's **angle of repose** or nothing
runs down it. With 35° repose the slant is set at 40° — the shallowest angle
that still runs, because every extra degree is expensive:

> A single slant across **both** bores is inherently tall. At 40° it rises 5.13
> across the pair. The plunger works entirely *below* the middle wall, so that
> rise has to fit under the wall, and the stroke has to fit above the rise.
> Every degree of slant is therefore paid for twice.

## The cycle

The plunger reciprocates between two hard stops. Neither is a number that was
chosen — both are things the machine runs into:

| | limit | what stops it |
|---|---|---|
| **TOP** | plunger's **top face**, where it passes **under** the wall, meets the wall's bottom edge | the middle wall is the stopper |
| **BOTTOM** | plunger's **low end** reaches the tubes' open rim | any lower and the seal breaks and material runs out |

Two details about the top stop that are easy to get wrong:

- Contact is **not** at the plunger's high end. That end is out at B's far
  side, past the wall entirely, and never touches it.
- Contact is on the plunger's **top** face, not its underside.

Since the face rises left to right, of the two points beneath the wall it is
the one at the wall's **right** face that stands highest and touches first.
That single point sets the whole stroke.

### What the material does

**Down-stroke.** Material comes across from B and slides down the face.

**Up-stroke.** The load splits. What sits high on the face — the B side — falls
back into B. What has already slid low — the A side — is carried up and
delivered into A.

So each stroke *sorts* rather than pushes: some returns, some crosses. The net
is a transfer B → A. Nothing has to be timed.

## The screen is the whole problem

**This mechanism only works if the screen does something a plain mesh does
not.** It has to resist flow downward under gravity, yet permit flow upward
when driven by the plunger. Those demands are in tension, and a plain woven
mesh satisfies neither well: sized to hold material under gravity it also
blocks it under stroke pressure; sized to pass it under pressure it lets it
drain back.

### It must yield to force, not to motion

A subtlety that is easy to get wrong, and that the simulation got wrong first.

The obvious way to model a one-way screen is *let a particle through if it is
moving upward*. That is not the mechanism, and it flatters it badly. It means
material passes **because it is flying** — so fast, bouncing particles sail
through while a slowly, steadily pressed column is blocked. That is backwards
from the real loading case.

A real machine of this kind moves slowly. Nothing is flung anywhere. Material
goes through because the plunger *forces* it through. The physical quantity is
**contact force**, not velocity.

Under the velocity rule 403 of 542 particles were delivered; under a force
threshold, 235. The velocity rule was letting material through for free.

### Slower is better — which is the point

| stroke | delivered | fell out |
|---|---|---|
| fast | 235 / 542 | 10 |
| **slow** | **315 / 542** | **3** |

Slowing the machine by 2.2× delivered 43% more and lost a third as much. Under
the old velocity rule the result would have gone the other way.

At a slow stroke the material stays a **packed column** against the face, so
the plunger transmits load into it steadily and the contact force at the screen
builds and *stays* above yield. A fast stroke throws the pack apart — particles
go ballistic, lose contact, and the load spikes and collapses instead of
pressing.

**So this is a slow, force-driven machine, and it should be built and run as
